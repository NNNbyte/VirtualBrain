Ayna · research on virtual patients, not clinical advice
About fifty million people live with epilepsy, and for one in three, medication doesn't stop the seizures. Their doctors face a hard choice: surgery, which can cure but only if the right tissue is found and removed, or a stimulator, which is safer but rarely stops seizures. You can't try both on the same person. A virtual-brain twin promises exactly that rehearsal, but nobody yet knows when to trust one. That is the problem we took on.
How Ayna works
Judging: Decentralized agency · 20%

Seven AI agents with different powers. No agent grades its own work.

Sealed answer key

Only the Checker can open it, and only after predictions are frozen.

Failed check → repair

Everything built on a rejected result is marked stale and redone.

Needs board

Agents post what they're missing; any eligible agent can claim it.

A lead agent proposes tasks but cannot approve results. In both studies the plan changed when checks failed.

Roles, permissions and budgets: system/profiles/*.json · procedures: .claude/skills/*/SKILL.md
Ayna is a team of seven Claude agents with different powers. A scout reads the literature, a curator prepares patient data, a modeller builds the brain twin on a cloud machine, a designer simulates surgery and stimulation, a checker re-derives every number and is the only one who can open the answer key, and a reviewer signs off. A lead proposes work, but plain code, the controller, decides: no agent approves its own work, the answer key stays sealed until predictions are frozen, and when a check fails everything downstream is repaired. Agents also post needs that others pick up.

And it works end to end. Study one, on real patients, in six hours: 32 checked literature claims, 10 patients curated without peeking at outcomes, and the finding that the published brain-twin pipeline was not qualified, with four code bugs. So it stopped instead of guessing. Study two used a blinded virtual patient: the answer key was sealed, a twin was fitted, 18 treatments were simulated, predictions were frozen, and only then was the key opened. Result: we missed the true onset zone, and the agents reported that honestly; the best treatment is not determinable. Across both studies, 134 independent checks, 27 rejections and 11 repairs.

comparison/comparison.md · single-agent answer hashed before the truth was read
Does the collective add anything? We gave one Claude agent the same task and scored both systems on seven criteria that were written and hashed before the run. The single agent took two minutes and ranked the true zone fourth, better than our collective. But it found none of the four known bugs, never tested its method, and recommended stimulating three regions that miss the true zone, with no warning. The collective missed too, but caught every bug, rejected its own models, and said the answer was not determinable. Eighteen versus six out of twenty-one. The collective's edge is not a better guess; it is knowing when a guess can't be trusted.
