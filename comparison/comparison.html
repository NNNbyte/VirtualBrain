Where the single agent did as well or better

  • Localisation. A regularised gain-matrix inversion ranked the true region (column 15, Left-F1-lateral-prefrontal) 4th, inside its spread zone. Its declared EZ (top 3) still has recall 0.
  • The multi-agent's frozen Bayesian estimates had recall 0 in every window, with the true region ranked 46th–159th. Only its exploratory hypothesis H1 listed the region, as a propagation candidate.
  • Speed. About 115 times faster, and it used only 2 of its 15 minutes.
  • It found that the gain matrix is badly conditioned (condition number about 5e6) and flagged possible cross-midline leakage.

Where the multi-agent design paid off

  • It found all 4 defects: the likelihood skip, the sampling rate (125 Hz, not 250), the double log and non-convergence (R-hat up to 6.4). It also found that the seizure is stimulation-induced.
  • Two synthetic benchmarks failed, so it rejected its own models and called them "not qualified".
  • Blinding was enforced: predictions frozen at ledger seq 205, vault opened at seq 214, with hashes.
  • R5 reproduced the simulations bit for bit. It made no recommendation, because the truth showed none could be verified.

Bottom line

In this test, the multi-agent design did not localise the onset zone better. A 2-minute heuristic came closer than the 4-hour Bayesian pipeline. The single agent, though, recommended stimulating three regions that do not include the true one, based on circular simulations, and nothing in its output warned the reader.

What the multi-agent design adds is knowing when its answer can't be trusted: it caught the defects, rejected the models that failed validation, enforced blinding and made the results reproducible. The cost is about 100 times the time. Next step: add the cheap source-imaging baseline to the pipeline as a benchmarked comparator.

Caveats: one patient and one baseline run. The baseline had no web access. Study-002 review tasks were still running at scoring time.
